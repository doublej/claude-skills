# Premise Lab

The premise is the engine. Rewriting punchlines on top of a blurry premise is
wasted effort — this is the most common way humor work fails.

<formula>

```
topic + attitude → premise
```

A **topic** is a subject area. A **premise** is a specific claim about it that has
a point of view. The attitude is what converts one into the other.

Weak: "Dating apps are a thing people use."
Working: "Dating apps are exhausting because romance now feels like unpaid admin work."

</formula>

<attitude_vocabulary>

When stuck, rotate these against the topic. Ask literally: *what is [word] about X?*

hard · weird · scary · stupid · embarrassing · unfair · fake · exhausting · petty ·
absurd · humiliating · grim · desperate · smug · delusional · shameless · tedious ·
cruel · pathetic · baffling · insulting · pointless

**Negative and tense attitudes are easier to build on** — they create pressure, and
pressure is what the turn releases. This is not cynicism; it is mechanics. There
is nothing to relieve if nothing is clenched.

Generate six premises before judging any of them. Volume first, filter second —
filtering during generation kills the useful weird ones.

</attitude_vocabulary>

<worked_example>

Topic: **food delivery**

| Attitude | Premise candidate |
|---|---|
| hard | The app turns hunger into logistics management. |
| weird | A stranger knows your dinner routine better than your parents do. |
| stupid | You pay a service fee for the privilege of inconveniencing everyone. |
| scary | How fast a person becomes emotionally dependent on a scooter icon. |

Then pick the one with the most **personal pressure**, not the cleverest phrasing.
Build from that single premise — not from the topic.

</worked_example>

<quality_filter>

Run *after* generating, never during.

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Specific | Is it narrow enough to see? |
| Attitude | Is there a stance, or is this description? |
| Observation | Is it a claim, not a timeline of events? |
| Recognizable | Would a stranger recognize the pattern? |
| Single | One point of view, or several topics tangled? |
| Generative | Can it produce five different jokes, or exactly one? |

The last test is the one people skip. A premise that yields exactly one joke is a
joke wearing a premise costume — fine for a one-liner, fatal for a bit.

</quality_filter>

<specificity_ladder>

Broad topics cannot be joked about; they can only be gestured at. Climb down until
you hit something with edges.

```
transport → public transport → the subway → the security check →
the rush-hour bag scanner → the guy who takes out his laptop wrong every day
```

Stop when you reach a scene, a person, an object, or a behavior. If you cannot
picture it, keep descending.

</specificity_ladder>

<personal_to_universal>

The most common premise failure: the writer is still inside their own story.

**Symptom:** the premise is dense with *I / me / my* and requires biography.

**Fix:** state the recognizable social pattern first, then reintroduce your
specific detail as evidence.

Before: "My mother texts me every day asking if I've eaten."
After: "There's an age where your mother stops asking about your career and starts
asking whether you've eaten — mine made that switch the week I got promoted."

The audience agrees to the pattern, *then* receives the specific. Reverse the
order and they are politely waiting for you to explain why this matters.

</personal_to_universal>

<anti_patterns>

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Story mistaken for premise | "Yesterday I went to the supermarket…" is a timeline, not a point |
| No attitude | A neutral fact is a lecture |
| Over-private | Only you know why it matters |
| Premise contains its own example | Too narrow to generate more than the one joke |
| Several topics at once | The audience does not know what they are agreeing to |

</anti_patterns>
