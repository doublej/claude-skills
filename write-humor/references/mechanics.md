# Mechanics

<why_things_are_funny>

Four theories. They are not rivals — they are four levers on the same machine.
Most working jokes pull two or three at once.

| Theory | Mechanism | You reach for it when |
|---|---|---|
| **Incongruity** (core) | Expectation is built, then broken by something that still fits | Always. This is the engine; the rest are modifiers. |
| **Superiority** | Laughter at someone's smallness — safest when the target is your past self | Self-deprecation, roasting, status comedy |
| **Relief** | Tension is built deliberately, then discharged | Taboo, grief, workplace dread, anything the reader is already clenched about |
| **Benign violation** | A norm is broken *and* the break is signalled as safe | Anything with teeth. The safety net is what makes it legal. |

Kant's formulation of incongruity is still the most useful sentence on the subject:
*"a strained expectation suddenly reduced to nothing."* Strained is the operative
word. A weak setup cannot produce a strong turn.

**Benign violation is the calibration dial.** Too little violation and it is a
pleasantry. Too much and the reader flinches instead of laughing. The safety net
is supplied by one of: self-implication (you are also the target), impossible
exaggeration (nobody thinks this is literal), explicit fiction (a scenario, not a
person), or punching up (target has more power than the audience).

</why_things_are_funny>

<two_story_model>

The single most useful diagnostic in comedy writing. Every working joke contains
two stories occupying the same words.

```
Setup ──────────────► Story 1   (what the audience assumes)
                          │
                    [connector]  ← the element that supports both readings
                          │
Punch ──────────────► Story 2   (what the punch reveals, and it still fits)
```

If you cannot name Story 1 and Story 2 separately, you do not have a joke — you
have a statement with an attitude.

**The connector is the hinge.** It is the word, object, rule, behavior, or image
that can carry more than one meaning. Punchlines are not invented; they are found
by interrogating the connector.

</two_story_model>

<joke_mine>

The drill for turning a setup into punches. Run it literally — write the answers
down, do not do it in your head.

1. **What does the setup make the reader assume?** List every assumption, not just
   the obvious one.
2. **Which exact assumption should break?** Name one. Specificity here decides
   whether the punch lands or merely surprises.
3. **What created that assumption?** A word, an object, a behavior, a rule. This
   is the connector.
4. **What else could the connector mean?** List five. The first two will be
   obvious; the useful ones are usually four and five.
5. **What second story makes that alternate meaning true?** The reading must be
   *retroactively inevitable*, not merely different.
6. **What is the shortest speakable line that reveals it?**

Produce three punch directions, not one finished line. Voice decides which
survives; mechanics decide which are viable.

**Worked example**

Premise: *online meetings are fake-professional because everyone is performing
office behavior inside a private mess.*

| Step | Answer |
|---|---|
| Setup | "On Zoom, everyone says 'let's align'…" |
| Story 1 | Adults are coordinating work |
| Target assumption | Their environment supports professionalism |
| Connector | "align" — business alignment, or physically arranging a room |
| Reinterpretations | aligning the laptop to hide laundry · aligning the camera so the bed disappears · aligning your face while the doorbell goes |
| Story 2 | Corporate language is a survival technique for concealing domestic chaos |

</joke_mine>

<escalation>

A single joke is one turn. Longer pieces need structure, and there are exactly
three ways to extend without repeating yourself.

**Tags** — additional laughs off the *same* connector, each shorter than the last.
The cheapest real estate in comedy: you already paid for the setup. If a joke
lands and you move on, you left tags on the table.

**Act-out** — stop reporting the scene and perform it. Convert "my landlord was
being unreasonable" into the landlord's actual sentence. Use when a draft has gone
explanatory. The rule: act out what reveals the premise, not what demonstrates
range.

**Mix** — move the logic of one world into another. Dating logic into job
interviews. Corporate language into family dinners. Childhood rules into adult
bureaucracy. Mix generates premises when observation has run dry.

**Callback** — a planted element returns where it is not expected. Three-phase:

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| Plant | Reads as an ordinary laugh. Do not signal it. |
| Ferment | Enough material passes that the reader stops holding it |
| Recover | Returns with full context attached — the laugh is bigger than the plant |

A callback that the reader sees coming is a repetition. The plant must earn its
own small laugh or it reads as setup and gets remembered.

</escalation>

<rewrite_ladder>

In order. Do not skip to step 6 — most "this isn't landing" problems die at 1–3.

1. Clarify the premise
2. Cut the setup
3. Move the reveal later
4. Replace explanation with image or action
5. Add act-out or mix
6. Add tags
7. Read it aloud
8. Test it on someone

**Word economy:** cut every word that does not build the assumption, sharpen the
attitude, create rhythm, or land the turn. Nothing else has a job.

**Final position is load-bearing.** The decisive word or image goes last unless
you have a deliberate rhythm reason. Everything after the reveal is drag.

</rewrite_ladder>
