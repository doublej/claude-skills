# Device Catalog

Twelve named moves. Each entry: what it does, when to reach for it, how it fails.

Devices are not a menu to work through — pick the one that matches the pressure
already in the material. If none fits, the premise is the problem, not the device.

---

<expectation_violation>

## 1. Expectation violation

The base device; everything else is a variation. Build a familiar frame, reinforce
it, then break it.

The reader's mind runs ahead on the **least-effort path**. You win by making that
path wrong — while leaving a second path that was always there.

**Layering.** A single break is a joke. Three breaks is a bit:
1. First turn — reader accepts it
2. Second turn — reader laughs
3. Third turn — reader goes quiet, because the third turn is true

That third beat is where comedy becomes writing. Use it when the premise has real
pressure underneath.

**Fails when:** the turn surprises but does not fit (random ≠ funny), or the frame
was never solidly built (nothing to break).

</expectation_violation>

<personality_contrast>

## 2. Personality contrast

A character becomes funny in the gap between *what they are* and *what they do*.
Stack three mismatches and a flat label becomes a person:

| Layer | Mismatch |
|---|---|
| Identity | The label deflates — "he was a coal baron, now he's a guy from Ohio" |
| Behavior | Actions escalate past the frame — "bought a new car, a new house, a new wife" |
| Aspiration | What they want contradicts what they are |

**Fails when:** you only have one mismatch (reads as a single joke, not a
character), or the contrasts are arbitrary rather than revealing.

</personality_contrast>

<environment_mismatch>

## 3. Environment mismatch

Put the right thing in the wrong place. Then — and this is the whole device —
**narrate the absurdity as though it were normal.**

The comedy is not in the mismatch. It is in the deadpan. A child doing homework in
a nightclub is a situation; a child explaining that the nightclub *improved his
handwriting* is a joke.

**Fails when:** you signal that you know it is strange. The moment the narrator
winks, the reader stops doing the work, and the work was the laugh.

</environment_mismatch>

<detail_stacking>

## 4. Detail stacking

Replace the abstraction with things the reader can see. Specific nouns beat
adjectives every time.

"The office was depressing" → "there was a motivational poster from 2011 and a
fridge nobody had opened since the reorg."

Stack three to five concrete details and the reader builds the scene themselves —
which means they arrive at your conclusion believing they thought of it.

**Fails when:** the details are generic (they must be *these* details, not any
details), or you stack past five and it becomes inventory.

</detail_stacking>

<social_observation>

## 5. Social observation

Route a private story through a public pattern. The personal detail is evidence;
the pattern is what the audience agrees to.

Order matters: **pattern first, then your specifics.** Lead with the private
detail and the reader has no reason to care yet.

**Fails when:** it stays a diary entry (only you know why it matters) or flattens
into a complaint everyone has already heard.

</social_observation>

<callback>

## 6. Callback

See `mechanics.md` → escalation. Plant, ferment, recover.

**Fails when:** the plant is too loud (reader holds it, sees the return coming) or
the recovery adds no new context (repetition, not escalation).

</callback>

<self_deprecation>

## 7. Self-deprecation

Superiority theory, aimed inward. The safest possible target is your own past
self, because the audience gets the status bump without the cruelty.

The advanced form is **self-deprecation that indicts the reader**: make yourself
the specimen, then reveal the reader is the same specimen.

**Fails when:** it becomes an actual bid for reassurance. The audience must
believe you are fine, or the laugh curdles into concern.

</self_deprecation>

<act_out>

## 8. Act-out

Stop describing, start performing. Give the person their actual words, their exact
gesture, the specific pause.

Reported: "customer service was useless."
Acted: "'I completely understand your frustration' — said four times, by four
different people, each of whom then transferred me."

**Fails when:** the act-out demonstrates performance rather than revealing the
premise, or it runs past its own ending.

</act_out>

<mix>

## 9. Mix

Import the logic of one domain into another. Generative when observation is dry —
you can manufacture premises by pairing domains at random and asking what
transfers.

**Fails when:** the two worlds have no genuine structural rhyme, making it a
costume rather than an insight.

</mix>

<misdirection_by_precision>

## 10. Misdirection by precision

An unexpectedly exact number, name, or measurement implies a whole history the
reader has to imagine.

"I've been asked to leave three separate Applebee's" is funnier than "I get thrown
out of restaurants" — the specificity smuggles in an entire backstory.

**Fails when:** the precision is not load-bearing. Random numbers are noise; the
number must imply something.

</misdirection_by_precision>

<escalation_to_absurdity>

## 11. Escalation to absurdity

Start plausible, advance by defensible steps, arrive somewhere insane. Each step
must be *locally* reasonable — the absurdity is only visible looking back.

This is also the primary safety net for benign violation: an escalation that
reaches the impossible cannot be mistaken for a claim.

**Fails when:** you jump to absurd in one step (no ladder to climb) or the steps
stop being individually defensible.

</escalation_to_absurdity>

<anticlimax>

## 12. Anticlimax

Build weight, then deliberately underdeliver. Inverse of escalation: the violation
is the *absence* of the promised payoff.

Requires real tension first. Works well as a closer, because the deflation reads
as honesty rather than failure.

**Fails when:** the buildup was not genuinely tense, so there is nothing to
puncture — it just reads as a joke that did not arrive.

</anticlimax>
